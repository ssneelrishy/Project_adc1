01 — SIGNAL PATH

Six stages carry the sound from the phone's audio circuitry to the receiver's cone. Nothing here is proprietary — every part is a standard, low-cost analog component.

1 / AUDIO SOURCE

Phone headphone output

The 3.5 mm jack (or a USB-C DAC dongle) outputs a line-level audio signal, roughly 0.1–1 V peak-to-peak. This becomes the baseband waveform that gets carried on the radio frequency — it never touches the antenna directly. This is the trace drawn in the TX scope above.

3.5mm TRS cableline-out ~ -10 dBV
2 / MODULATOR

FM transmitter circuit

A coupling capacitor blocks DC and feeds the audio into a varactor diode sitting inside an LC tank circuit. Audio voltage bends the varactor's capacitance, which shifts the tank's resonant frequency in step with the sound — that shifting is the FM modulation. A transistor stage (Colpitts/Hartley oscillator, or a single-chip driver like the BH1417) keeps the oscillation running at whatever centre frequency the trimmer capacitor sets, and boosts it for radiation.

3 / RADIATOR

Transmit antenna

A short wire — ideally a quarter-wavelength, about 75 cm for this band, though hobby builds often trim it shorter — turns the oscillating current into a radiated electromagnetic wave at the transmitter's set frequency. Antenna length and power stage together set the usable range, typically a few metres to a few tens of metres at legal unlicensed power.

~75 cm wire9V or 5V supply
4 / CHANNEL

Open-air propagation

The carrier travels through free space at whatever frequency the transmitter is set to. It arrives at every FM radio within range, but only means something to a receiver whose tuner is set to that same number — every other receiver just sees it as noise on some other channel.

5 / RECEIVER

Any standard FM radio

This is the part you don't have to build — it's already in every FM radio, car stereo, or radio-enabled phone. Its antenna picks up whatever carriers are in range, and its tuner/local- oscillator stage is turned by hand (or by app) until it lands on the transmitter's exact frequency. Only then does the demodulator (discriminator or PLL) reverse the modulation and hand back the original waveform sample-for-sample — a few tenths of a megahertz off, and it recovers nothing but its own noise floor, as the RX scope shows.

tuner + local osc.FM demodulator de-emphasis filter
6 / OUTPUT

Built-in audio amplifier + speaker

The recovered audio passes through the radio's own amplifier IC and out through its own cone. When the two dials above agree, the trace coming out here is not just similarly shaped — it is the transmitter's waveform, redrawn. From the listener's perspective, the FM radio has just become a wireless speaker for the phone.

02 — DESIGN NOTES

WHY THE TRACES MATCH

A radio only demodulates the carrier its tuner is currently sitting on. Once both ends agree on a frequency, the receiver isn't approximating the transmitted sound — it's physically recovering the same modulation, so the waveform it hands to the speaker is, sample for sample, the one that left the phone.

RANGE & POWER

Keep transmit power low (well under 100 mW at the antenna) — most regions restrict unlicensed FM transmission to a short range by law, which is what keeps a school/DIY build legal without a broadcast licence.

FREQUENCY CHOICE

Pick a gap in the local FM dial (a frequency with no strong nearby station) for the trimmer capacitor to sit on, so the receiver locks onto your signal cleanly instead of fighting a broadcaster.

STEREO OPTION

Swapping the single-transistor oscillator for a stereo encoder IC (e.g. BH1417) adds a 19 kHz pilot tone so receivers unlock stereo separation, at the cost of a slightly more complex build.
